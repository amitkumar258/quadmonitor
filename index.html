Analysis

Cooperation by Domain

Note: some initiatives span more than one domain and are counted in each.

Strategic Portfolio and Scope

  • Thirty-nine initiatives across seven summit cycles (2021 to 2026), most recently reviewed at the Manila Leaders' Summit (17 November 2026), mark a shift from a narrow maritime-security consultation to a broad programme spanning security, climate, infrastructure, health, education, and trade.
  • Security leads with twenty-four initiatives across ten sub-domains, five in maritime and eight in critical and emerging technology, confirming the Quad has dispersed rather than concentrated its hard-power ambitions.
  • Climate (8) is the most ambitious public-goods agenda, spanning green shipping, clean hydrogen, ocean research, climate information services, and supply-chain diversification.
  • Infrastructure (5) offers a credible alternative to coercive connectivity financing and includes the Cable Connectivity partnership, its largest disclosed grant commitment (about $148 million).
  • Health (3) has produced the most measurable human-impact initiative, the Cancer Moonshot, while Education and Exchange (1) and the new Trade and Investment track (1), the Quad Business Leaders' Forum, remain nascent but strategically significant.

Cooperation by Type

Note: many initiatives have more than one cooperation type.

Cooperation Patterns

  • Capacity Building dominates with seventeen instances, nearly half the portfolio, reflecting a preference for institution-building over binding commitments or joint enforcement.
  • Interoperability (12) is equally central, reflecting sustained investment in aligning the procedures, standards, and systems of four sovereign partners who share no formal alliance obligations.
  • Resilience (4) and Standard Setting (7) signal an ambition to shape the region's technical and regulatory architecture through influence rather than mandate.
  • Data Sharing (5) and Monitoring (4) prioritise shared situational awareness over collective enforcement.
  • Supply Chain Resilience (7) has gained significant momentum, driven by three dedicated semiconductor initiatives alongside broader infrastructure and trade programmes, reflecting the Quad's deepening focus on strategic technology resilience.

Funding Architecture and Scale

  • The disclosed picture splits into three tiers, and the gap between them is itself the finding. The Quad's largest numbers are its softest commitments.
  • Pledges and mobilisation targets total roughly 70 billion dollars: the over 50 billion dollar Quality Infrastructure pledge and the up to 20 billion dollar Critical Minerals target. Neither is yet backed by committed or allocated funding.
  • Loan facilities form a second tier of roughly 4.3 billion dollars, dominated by Japan's 3.3 billion dollar JICA COVID-19 Crisis Response Emergency Support Loan programme and 800 million dollars in US DFC facilities.
  • Aid and grants total roughly 9.9 billion dollars, but this figure is dominated by COVID-era multilateral health flows: COVAX (5.6 billion), Last-Mile delivery (2 billion), and CEPI (524 million) account for over 8.1 billion of it.
  • Setting those three aside, discrete bilateral grants and aid (the money attributable to a single member and initiative) come to roughly 1.75 billion dollars across the entire portfolio, led by Australia's vaccine-era health flows.
  • The Quad's confirmed cash footprint therefore remains a small fraction of its headline figures.

Funding by Country, Domain, and Delivery

  • Australia leads individual member commitments at just over 1 billion dollars, including its 407 million dollar standing Pacific vaccine commitment folded into the Quad Vaccine Partnership. The United States follows at around 201 million dollars, then Japan at around 190 million dollars.
  • India's direct disclosed contributions stand at around 20 million dollars, lifted by its 17.5 million dollar Cancer Moonshot commitment. Its in-kind contributions (notably vaccine manufacturing through Biological E.) remain a significant non-cash input that the dollar figures understate.
  • Collectively attributed money (commitments made by all four members jointly) comes to roughly 8.4 billion dollars, almost all of it COVID-era multilateral health aid.
  • By domain, Health dominates at roughly 9.5 billion dollars. Security follows at around 226 million dollars, then Climate (~156 million), Infrastructure (~148 million), and Education and Exchange (~10.5 million).
  • COVAX (5.6 billion), Last-Mile delivery (2 billion), and CEPI (524 million) are confirmed multilateral aid contributions, not aspirational pledges: government sources confirm these funds were released and disbursed.
  • Disbursement reporting remains the biggest transparency gap. Where audits exist (the US DFC loan to Biological E., Japan's JICA emergency loans) they confirm delivery, but most commitments still lack public disbursement data, making independent value-for-money assessment difficult.

Important Caveats First, the disaster relief responses for Myanmar, Papua New Guinea, and Vietnam are one-off deployments under the Humanitarian Assistance and Disaster Relief Mechanism rather than standing initiatives. They appear here as funding commitments and not as separate entries on the Initiatives page. Second, the Quality Infrastructure and Critical Minerals figures are aspirational pledges and mobilisation targets, not committed spending. The large COVAX, Last-Mile, and CEPI figures are confirmed multilateral contributions, but they bundle the four members' money and, in the Cancer Moonshot's case, philanthropic co-funding alongside government commitments. Third, government sources rarely publish disbursement data, so a committed figure should not be read as money that has already been spent.

About the Quad Monitor

The Quad is often criticised for being dormant, even declared dead, for lapsing into summitry, and for drifting toward soft, non-security issues. Sceptics argue it has done too little to mount a credible challenge or alternative to China, falling short of the objectives it was founded to pursue.

The Quad Monitor is an attempt to cut through that debate using empirical data. It maps every Quad initiative across seven summit cycles, tracking where the grouping cooperates, what forms that cooperation takes, how much capital is committed and how little is verifiably disbursed, and how each initiative has performed against its stated goals. Every figure is drawn from official government sources.

Alongside the initiatives, the Monitor records the full institutional calendar: leaders' summits, foreign ministers' meetings, sherpa consultations and the working-group mechanisms, together with the financial commitments disclosed against each line of effort.

Methodology

How Ratings Work

Each initiative is scored on four measurable metrics. Each metric runs from 0 to 4 and carries equal weight. The rating is the average of the four scores, shown on a four-star scale. No subjective labels are used.

CapitalOne point for each "yes" to four questions: Was money committed? Is it adequate to the initiative's own stated targets (an aspirational mobilisation figure, or a fraction of the target, counts as no)? Has it been released? Has it been spent? The size of the sum does not matter, since needs differ by initiative. Initiatives that set no funding target of their own, because they work through coordination, existing national assets or private capital, are marked not applicable and averaged over the other three metrics.
Mentions in joint statementsThe share of Leaders' Summit and Foreign Ministers' Meeting joint statements issued after launch that name the initiative or describe its specific work. A topic listed in passing does not count. 4: 75% or more; 3: 50 to 74%; 2: 25 to 49%; 1: below 25%; 0: never mentioned after launch.
Working-group meetingsHow regularly the Quad working group that carries the initiative meets, scored on the record of the nine working groups on the Working Groups page. 4: two or more dated meetings in the past year; 3: met in the past year; 2: last met 12 to 18 months ago; 1: last met more than 18 months ago; 0: no meeting on record, or the initiative sits outside all nine groups. Events with only an approximate date count toward when a group last met.
Tangible outcomesThe share of the initiative's stated objectives that have been met on the public record. 4: 75% or more; 3: 50 to 74%; 2: 25 to 49%; 1: some, below 25%; 0: none.

Stars. Average of 3.5 or more: four stars; 2.5 to 3.49: three stars; 1.5 to 2.49: two stars; below 1.5: one star. Initiatives launched too recently to have appeared in any later joint statement are shown as not yet rated.

A note on public data. Every score rests on what governments and implementing agencies have chosen to publish. Funding that is never disclosed, meetings that are never announced and results that are never reported cannot be counted, so an initiative may perform better than its rating suggests. Ratings are a comparative guide to the public record, not a final verdict, and are revised as new information appears. Corrections can be sent through Submit an Update.

Statement corpus: the seven Leaders' Summits (March 2021 to Manila, November 2026) and Foreign Ministers' Meetings from Melbourne (February 2022) to Atlanta (October 2026). Ratings as of 17 November 2026.
